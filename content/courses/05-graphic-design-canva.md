# Module: The principles of good design
Learn the rules that make designs look professional — whatever tool you use.

## Lesson: What is graphic design? (8 min)
**Graphic design** is the art of communicating ideas visually by combining text, images, colour and shapes. A good design isn't just pretty — it gets the right message to the right people quickly.

### Where you see graphic design every day
- Posters for church programmes, school events and concerts
- Product packaging on shop shelves
- Billboards and roadside signs
- Social media posts and WhatsApp status graphics
- Logos of banks, telecoms and football clubs
- Websites and app screens

### Design is communication
Before you open any design tool, answer three questions:

1. **Who** is this for? (students, parents, business owners…)
2. **What** must they understand or do? (attend an event, buy a product, call a number)
3. **Where** will they see it? (a phone screen, a printed A4 poster, a large banner)

A poster for a youth coding workshop and a funeral announcement need very different designs, even though both share dates and venues.

### The design process
1. **Brief** — understand the goal, audience, content and deadline.
2. **Research** — collect inspiration (Pinterest, Behance, Canva templates).
3. **Sketch** — quick rough layouts on paper. Five ugly sketches beat one perfect one.
4. **Design** — build it in your tool.
5. **Feedback** — show someone from the target audience.
6. **Refine and deliver** — fix issues and export the right file.

> [!TIP]
> Professionals collect designs they like in a "swipe file". Start a folder of screenshots of posters and posts that catch your eye, and note *why* each one works.

## Lesson: Hierarchy, contrast and alignment (10 min)
Three principles do most of the work in any good design.

### Visual hierarchy — what do people see first?
Hierarchy guides the viewer's eye in order of importance. On an event poster the order might be:

1. **Event name** — biggest and boldest
2. **Date, time and venue** — clear and easy to find
3. **Details** — speakers, activities
4. **Call to action / contact** — "Register: 024 000 0000"

Create hierarchy with **size**, **weight** (bold vs regular), **colour**, and **position** (top and centre are seen first).

> [!WARNING]
> If everything is big and bold, nothing stands out. A common beginner mistake is making all text the same size — or every line a different colour.

### Contrast — make important things different
Contrast means making elements clearly different so they stand out:

- **Colour contrast:** dark text on a light background (or the reverse).
- **Size contrast:** a huge headline with small body text.
- **Weight contrast:** a bold heading with light body text.
- **Shape contrast:** one circle among squares.

Contrast is also about **readability**. Light yellow text on a white background is almost invisible — especially for people with weak eyesight or when printed on a cheap printer.

### Alignment — line things up
Every element should line up with something else. Invisible lines create order and make designs feel professional.

- **Left alignment** is easiest to read for paragraphs.
- **Centre alignment** works for short headlines and formal designs but is hard to read for long text.
- Don't mix many alignments in one design — choose one main alignment.

Use the guides and snapping in Canva: the pink lines that appear when you drag an element show it's aligned.

> Take a cluttered flyer (or find one online). Redesign it on paper with a clear hierarchy: one dominant headline, then key details, then contact information, all left-aligned.

## Lesson: Repetition, proximity and white space (9 min)
### Repetition — create consistency
Repeating colours, fonts, shapes or styles ties a design together and builds a recognisable **brand**. If your headings are green and bold on page one, keep them green and bold on every page, post and poster.

### Proximity — group related things
Items that belong together should be close together; unrelated items should be further apart. On a flyer, keep the date, time and venue as one group, and the contact details as another. Readers then understand the structure without effort.

### White space — let the design breathe
**White space** (or *negative space*) is the empty area around and between elements. It doesn't have to be white — it's simply space without content.

Beginners often try to fill every corner. Professionals do the opposite:

- Space makes designs look **premium and confident**.
- It improves **readability**.
- It directs **attention** to what matters.

Think of luxury brands: lots of space, few words.

### Balance
- **Symmetrical balance** — equal weight on both sides; formal and stable (certificates, invitations).
- **Asymmetrical balance** — different elements balance each other (a big photo on the left balanced by text on the right); dynamic and modern.

### The squint test
Squint at your design until it's blurry. Can you still see the most important element? Are the groups clear? If everything blurs into one grey mass, increase contrast and spacing.

> [!TIP]
> When a design feels "wrong" but you don't know why, try removing something. Often less is more.

# Module: Typography and colour
Choose fonts and colours that look great and communicate clearly.

## Lesson: Typography basics (10 min)
**Typography** is the art of arranging text. Because most designs are mostly text, typography often decides whether a design looks amateur or professional.

### Font categories
| Category | Looks like | Feels | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serif** | Small "feet" on letters (e.g. Times, Playfair, Cormorant) | Traditional, trustworthy, elegant | Certificates, invitations, formal documents |
| **Sans-serif** | Clean, no feet (e.g. Inter, Montserrat, Poppins) | Modern, friendly, clear | Social posts, apps, body text on screens |
| **Script** | Handwriting style (e.g. Great Vibes) | Personal, elegant | Names on certificates, wedding cards — sparingly! |
| **Display** | Decorative, bold | Fun, loud | Big headlines only |

### Golden rules
1. **Use two fonts at most** — one for headings, one for body text. A single font family in different weights also works beautifully.
2. **Pair contrasting fonts** — a bold sans-serif headline with a clean serif body, or vice versa. Two similar fonts together look like a mistake.
3. **Body text must be readable** — never use script or display fonts for paragraphs.
4. **Size matters** — on a social post, the smallest text should still be readable on a phone screen without zooming.
5. **Line spacing** — give lines of body text breathing room (about 1.4–1.6× the font size).
6. **Avoid ALL CAPS** for long text — it's hard to read. Short labels in caps with extra letter spacing can look elegant.

### Good font pairings to try in Canva
- **Montserrat** (headings) + **Open Sans** (body)
- **Playfair Display** (headings) + **Lato** (body)
- **Poppins** Bold + Poppins Regular
- **Anton** (impact headlines) + **Roboto** (body)

> [!WARNING]
> Stretching or squashing text to fit a space distorts the letters. Change the font size instead.

## Lesson: Colour theory and palettes (10 min)
Colour creates mood, draws attention and builds recognition.

### The colour wheel
- **Primary colours:** red, yellow, blue.
- **Complementary colours:** opposite on the wheel (blue & orange, red & green). High contrast and energetic.
- **Analogous colours:** next to each other (blue, teal, green). Calm and harmonious.
- **Monochromatic:** different shades and tints of one colour. Elegant and simple.

### Colour psychology (in general)
| Colour | Often suggests |
|---|---|
| Green | Growth, nature, health, money |
| Blue | Trust, calm, technology, banks |
| Red | Energy, urgency, passion, sales |
| Yellow / gold | Optimism, attention, luxury (gold) |
| Purple | Creativity, royalty |
| Black | Power, elegance, luxury |

Meanings vary by culture and context. In Ghana, red and black are traditional funeral colours, while white often signals celebration and victory — so think about your audience!

### The 60–30–10 rule
A simple formula for balanced colour:
- **60%** dominant/neutral colour (often white, cream or a dark background)
- **30%** secondary colour
- **10%** accent colour for buttons, highlights and the most important details

### Building a palette
1. Start from the **brand colour** or a photo you're using.
2. Use a palette generator like Coolors or Canva's colour palette tool.
3. Pick 1–2 main colours, 1 accent, plus a light and a dark neutral.
4. Save colour codes (e.g. `#03C14B`) so you can reuse them exactly.

### Accessibility
About 1 in 12 men have some form of colour blindness. Never use colour alone to show meaning (e.g. red/green for wrong/right) — add icons or labels. Check text contrast with a free contrast checker.

## Lesson: Images, icons and using them legally (8 min)
Strong images make designs come alive — but they must be good quality and used legally.

### Image quality
- Use **high-resolution** images. A blurry or pixelated photo instantly makes a design look cheap.
- For print, images should be at least **300 DPI** at the printed size.
- Avoid stretching images out of proportion — hold **Shift** (or use corner handles) to resize evenly.

### Choosing great photos
- Authentic over staged: real people in real settings (and local faces where possible — your audience wants to see themselves).
- One strong focal point.
- Leave space in the photo for text, or place text on a solid shape over the image.

### Text on images
Text placed directly on a busy photo is hard to read. Solutions:
- Add a semi-transparent dark or light **overlay** over the photo.
- Place text on a **solid shape** (rectangle or circle).
- Use the empty area of the photo (sky, wall).

### Using images legally
Just because an image is on Google doesn't mean you can use it. Copyright protects photos, illustrations and logos.

- ✅ Use **free stock** sites: Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay — free for most uses.
- ✅ Use Canva's **free** elements (paid ones show a crown icon).
- ✅ Take your own photos.
- ✅ Get **written permission** for photos of people, especially children, before using them in marketing.
- ❌ Don't copy other brands' logos or artwork.

### Icons and illustrations
Icons make information quick to scan (📍 venue, 📅 date, 📞 phone). Use icons from the **same style family** — mixing cartoon and outline icons looks messy.

# Module: Designing with Canva
Turn principles into real designs with a free, powerful tool.

## Lesson: Getting started with Canva (9 min)
**Canva** is a free online design tool used by millions of individuals and businesses. It runs in a web browser at canva.com and as an app on Android and iPhone. Students and teachers can apply for **Canva for Education**, and NGOs can apply for **Canva for Nonprofits** — both unlock premium features for free.

### Creating a design
1. Sign up with your email or Google account.
2. On the home page, search for a design type: *Instagram Post*, *Flyer (A4)*, *Poster*, *Logo*, *Presentation*. Canva sets the correct size automatically.
3. Or click **Custom size** and enter your own dimensions.

### The editor
- **Left panel** — Templates, Elements (shapes, icons, illustrations), Text, Uploads, Photos, Apps.
- **Canvas** — the middle area where you design.
- **Top toolbar** — changes depending on what's selected: font, colour, effects, position, transparency.

### Essential skills
- **Select** an element by clicking it; **Shift-click** to select several.
- **Group** elements (`Ctrl + G`) so they move together.
- **Align** and **distribute** with **Position** in the toolbar.
- **Layers:** use *Position → Forward/Backward* to bring things in front or behind.
- **Lock** an element so you don't move it by accident.
- **Duplicate** a page to create variations quickly.
- `Ctrl + Z` undo — your safety net.

### Templates: use them wisely
Templates are a great starting point, but thousands of people use the same ones. Always customise them: change the colours to your palette, swap the fonts, replace photos and rearrange the layout. A template should be a starting point, not the finished design.

> [!TIP]
> Turn on **View → Show rulers and guides** to align elements precisely, and use the **margins** setting to keep important content away from the edges.

## Lesson: Designing a flyer and social media posts (11 min)
Let's design a real flyer: **"Free Coding Workshop for Young People"**.

### Step 1 — Gather the content
- Headline: *Learn to Code — Free!*
- Sub-headline: *A 2-day beginner workshop in web development*
- Date & time: *Saturday 14 – Sunday 15 March, 9am – 1pm*
- Venue: *Ayeldu Community Centre*
- Call to action: *Register free: 024 000 0000*
- Logos of organisers and partners

### Step 2 — Choose the format
- **Print:** A4 or A3 portrait. Leave a margin of at least 5 mm from every edge.
- **WhatsApp status / Instagram story:** 1080 × 1920 px (vertical).
- **Instagram / Facebook post:** 1080 × 1080 px (square) or 1080 × 1350 px (portrait).

### Step 3 — Layout
- Put the **headline** in the top third in your boldest font.
- Use a strong photo of young people at computers (with permission!).
- Group **date, time and venue** together with small icons.
- Place the **call to action** at the bottom in a contrasting accent-colour box.
- Logos go in a neat row at the bottom — make them all a similar visual size.

### Step 4 — Design for the phone
Most people will see your design on a small screen while scrolling. Ask yourself:
- Can I read the headline in one second?
- Is the most important text big enough on a phone?
- Is there too much text? Cut ruthlessly — put extra details in the caption instead.

### Step 5 — Create a set
Use **Resize** (Canva Pro) or duplicate and adapt manually to create matching versions: a printed A4 flyer, a square post and a vertical status. Consistent colours, fonts and layout across them all builds recognition.

### Caption tips for social posts
The design grabs attention; the caption gives details. Start with a hook, give the key info, end with a clear action ("Tap the link to register 👇") and add 3–5 relevant hashtags.

## Lesson: Designing a simple logo and brand kit (10 min)
A **logo** is the face of a brand. A **brand kit** is the set of rules — logo, colours, fonts — that keeps every design consistent.

### What makes a good logo?
- **Simple** — recognisable at a glance and easy to remember.
- **Scalable** — works as a tiny app icon and on a huge banner.
- **Versatile** — works in full colour, in black and white, and on dark backgrounds.
- **Appropriate** — fits the business (a clinic shouldn't look like a nightclub).
- **Timeless** — avoids trendy effects that will look dated next year.

### Types of logos
- **Wordmark** — the name in a distinctive font (e.g. Google).
- **Lettermark** — initials (e.g. DBI).
- **Symbol / icon** — a picture (e.g. Apple).
- **Combination mark** — symbol plus name, like the DBI Learn logo, which combines a growth emblem with the name and tagline.

### Designing a logo in Canva
1. Write down the brand's name, personality (three words, e.g. *friendly, fresh, local*) and audience.
2. Sketch 10 ideas on paper first.
3. In Canva, start with a **Logo** design (500 × 500 px).
4. Choose one or two fonts and a simple shape or icon.
5. Test it small (zoom out until it's the size of a phone icon) and in black and white.
6. Download as **PNG with a transparent background** (Canva Pro) or ask for an **SVG** for printing.

> [!WARNING]
> Many Canva logo templates and elements can't be trademarked because others can use them too. For an important business, customise heavily or work with a designer on an original mark.

### The brand kit
Create a one-page brand guide including:
- Logo versions (full colour, white, black) and minimum size
- Colour palette with exact codes
- Heading and body fonts
- Example of a social post and a flyer using the brand

# Module: Professional workflow and portfolio
Export correctly, work with clients and build a portfolio that gets you hired.

## Lesson: Exporting for print and screen (8 min)
The same design must be saved differently depending on where it will be used.

### Screen vs print
| | Screen (social, web, WhatsApp) | Print (posters, flyers, banners) |
|---|---|---|
| Colour mode | **RGB** | **CMYK** (printers use cyan, magenta, yellow, black inks) |
| Resolution | 72–150 PPI is fine | **300 DPI** at final size |
| Formats | PNG, JPG, MP4, GIF | **PDF Print**, high-quality PNG |
| Extra | Keep files small for fast loading | Add **bleed** and crop marks |

### Bleed and safe area
Printers can't print exactly to the edge of the paper. **Bleed** is an extra 3 mm of background beyond the edge that gets trimmed off, so there are no white borders. In Canva: **File → Show print bleed**, extend backgrounds into the bleed, and keep text inside the margin (safe area). When downloading, choose **PDF Print** and tick **Crop marks and bleed**.

### Choosing formats for screen
- **PNG** — sharp text and graphics, supports transparency.
- **JPG** — smaller files for photo-heavy designs.
- **PDF Standard** — documents to share or read on screen.
- **MP4 / GIF** — animated designs.

### Talk to the printer
Before printing many copies, ask the print shop for their preferred file format and bleed size, and print one proof copy first. Colours on paper often look darker than on a bright phone screen.

## Lesson: Working with clients and feedback (8 min)
Design skills can earn you money — but working with clients needs good communication.

### Start with a clear brief
Ask:
- What is the goal of the design? What should people do after seeing it?
- Who is the audience?
- What text, logos and photos must be included?
- Where will it be used, and in what sizes?
- Are there brand colours or fonts?
- What is the **deadline** and the **budget**?
- Are there examples they like or dislike?

Write the answers down and send a short summary to the client so you both agree.

### Pricing and agreements
- Agree the price, number of **revision rounds** (e.g. two) and delivery date before you start.
- Many designers ask for a **deposit** (for example 50%) before starting.
- State what the client receives: final files in which formats.

### Presenting your work
- Show the design in context — a mockup of the flyer on a wall or the post on a phone screen.
- Explain *why* you made key choices ("The green connects to your brand and stands out on the poster board").
- Offer two or three directions for big projects, not ten.

### Handling feedback professionally
- Listen without taking it personally — feedback is about the design, not you.
- Ask questions: "When you say it should 'pop more', is it the headline or the colours?"
- Push back politely when a change hurts the design's goal, and explain why.
- Keep a record of each version (`flyer-v1`, `flyer-v2`).

> [!TIP]
> Under-promise and over-deliver. Delivering on time, every time, is the fastest way to get repeat clients and referrals.

## Lesson: Building your design portfolio (8 min)
A **portfolio** is a collection of your best work. For designers, it matters more than certificates or CVs — clients want to *see* what you can do.

### What to include
- **6–10 of your best pieces** — quality over quantity.
- **Variety:** a flyer, social media set, logo and brand kit, a presentation, maybe a certificate or menu.
- **Context for each piece:** the brief, your process (sketches, versions) and the result. Even practice projects count — label them "concept project".

### Where to show it
- **Behance** — a free platform owned by Adobe, popular with designers worldwide.
- **Instagram** — a dedicated account for your design work.
- **A simple website** — build one with skills from our Web Development course!
- **A PDF portfolio** — 8–12 pages you can attach to emails and WhatsApp.

### Getting your first projects
- Offer to redesign a poster for your school, church or a community group.
- Design for a family member's small business.
- Join design challenges on social media (e.g. 30-day design challenges).
- Volunteer for an NGO — real projects with real impact.

### Keep growing
- Study designs you admire and recreate them for practice (don't publish copies as your own).
- Learn advanced tools later: Adobe Illustrator, Photoshop, or free alternatives like Inkscape, GIMP and Figma.
- Join communities and ask for feedback regularly.
