# Module: Getting to know the computer
Understand what a computer is, its main parts, and how to use the keyboard and mouse comfortably.

## Lesson: What is a computer? (8 min)
A **computer** is an electronic machine that accepts information (input), works on it (processing), keeps it (storage) and gives you a result (output). That simple cycle — **input → process → output**, with **storage** alongside — describes every computer, from a giant server to the phone in your pocket.

### Computers are everywhere
You already use more computers than you think:

- A **smartphone** is a small, powerful computer with a touchscreen.
- An **ATM** at the bank is a computer that checks your card and PIN.
- A **mobile money** transaction is handled by computers in a data centre.
- A modern **car** contains dozens of tiny computers that control the engine and brakes.

### Hardware and software
Every computer has two sides:

| | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hardware** | The physical parts you can touch | Screen, keyboard, mouse, processor, battery |
| **Software** | The instructions that tell the hardware what to do | Windows, Android, Microsoft Word, WhatsApp, a web browser |

Hardware without software is like a body without thoughts; software without hardware has nowhere to run. They always work together.

### The operating system
The most important software is the **operating system (OS)**. It starts when you switch the device on and manages everything else: files, apps, the screen and the keyboard. Common operating systems are:

- **Windows** and **macOS** on laptops and desktops
- **Android** and **iOS** on phones and tablets
- **Linux**, used on many servers and also on some computers in schools

> [!TIP]
> When someone asks "What computer do you use?", they often mean "What operating system?". Knowing your OS helps you follow instructions and fix problems.

### Types of computers
- **Desktop** — sits on a desk, has a separate monitor, keyboard and mouse. Powerful and easy to repair.
- **Laptop** — portable, with a built-in screen, keyboard and battery.
- **Tablet** — a touchscreen device, lighter than a laptop.
- **Smartphone** — the computer most people in Ghana use every day.
- **Server** — a powerful computer that provides services (websites, email, mobile money) to many users over a network.

> [!TRY]
> Look around your home or classroom. List five devices that contain a computer. For each one, write down one input, one output and whether it stores information.

## Lesson: Inside the computer: input, output, processing and storage (10 min)
Let us open the box — not literally! — and see what each part does.

### Input devices
Input devices let you give information to the computer.

- **Keyboard** — for typing letters, numbers and commands
- **Mouse / touchpad** — for pointing, clicking and dragging
- **Touchscreen** — both input (you touch it) and output (it shows pictures)
- **Microphone** — records sound and voice
- **Camera / webcam** — captures pictures and video
- **Scanner** — turns paper documents into digital images

### Output devices
Output devices show or give you the result.

- **Monitor / screen** — displays text, images and video
- **Speakers / headphones** — play sound
- **Printer** — puts documents on paper
- **Projector** — shows the screen on a wall for a class or meeting

### Processing: the CPU
The **Central Processing Unit (CPU)**, also called the **processor**, is the "brain" of the computer. It follows instructions billions of times per second. Processor speed is measured in **gigahertz (GHz)**. A faster processor with more **cores** can do more work at the same time.

### Memory and storage — they are not the same!
This is where many beginners get confused.

| | RAM (memory) | Storage |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Holds what you are working on *right now* | Keeps files and apps *permanently* |
| When power goes off | Everything is lost | Everything stays |
| Typical size | 4 GB – 16 GB | 128 GB – 1 TB |
| Example | The table where you spread out your books while studying | The shelf where you keep all your books |

Storage comes in two main types:

- **HDD (Hard Disk Drive)** — spinning disks, cheaper, slower
- **SSD (Solid State Drive)** — no moving parts, much faster and more durable

### Units of storage
Computers count in **bytes**. One byte stores roughly one letter.

- 1 **KB** (kilobyte) ≈ 1,000 bytes — a short text message
- 1 **MB** (megabyte) ≈ 1,000 KB — a photo from a phone (2–5 MB)
- 1 **GB** (gigabyte) ≈ 1,000 MB — about an hour of standard video
- 1 **TB** (terabyte) ≈ 1,000 GB — hundreds of movies

> [!NOTE]
> When you buy a data bundle, it is measured in the same units. A 1 GB bundle can stream roughly one hour of standard-quality video — which is why downloading courses for offline study saves you money.

> [!TRY]
> On a computer, open **Settings → System → About** (Windows) or **Settings → About phone** (Android). Write down your device's RAM and storage.

## Lesson: Mastering the keyboard and mouse (9 min)
Good keyboard and mouse skills make everything faster. Professionals rarely hunt for keys — they use shortcuts and type without looking.

### Using the mouse
- **Click** (left button once) — select something
- **Double-click** — open a file or program
- **Right-click** — show a menu of options for that item
- **Click and drag** — move items or select text
- **Scroll wheel** — move up and down a page

On a laptop **touchpad**, tap with one finger to click and tap with two fingers to right-click.

### Important keys
- **Shift** — type capital letters and the top symbol on a key (e.g. `Shift + 2 = @`)
- **Caps Lock** — turns capital letters on and off
- **Enter** — start a new line or confirm
- **Backspace** — delete the character to the *left* of the cursor
- **Delete** — delete the character to the *right* of the cursor
- **Tab** — jump to the next box in a form
- **Esc** — cancel or close

### Shortcuts that save hours
Hold the first key, then press the second.

| Shortcut (Windows) | Mac | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| `Ctrl + C` | `Cmd + C` | Copy |
| `Ctrl + X` | `Cmd + X` | Cut |
| `Ctrl + V` | `Cmd + V` | Paste |
| `Ctrl + Z` | `Cmd + Z` | Undo your last action |
| `Ctrl + S` | `Cmd + S` | Save |
| `Ctrl + F` | `Cmd + F` | Find a word on the page |
| `Ctrl + A` | `Cmd + A` | Select all |
| `Alt + Tab` | `Cmd + Tab` | Switch between open programs |

> [!TIP]
> `Ctrl + Z` (undo) is your best friend. Deleted a paragraph by mistake? Press `Ctrl + Z` immediately.

### Typing properly
Place your fingers on the **home row**: left hand on `A S D F`, right hand on `J K L ;`. Feel the small bumps on `F` and `J` — they help you find the home row without looking. Each finger is responsible for the keys above and below its home key.

Start slowly and focus on accuracy. Speed comes with practice. A good goal for students is **30–40 words per minute** with few mistakes.

> [!TRY]
> Practise for 10 minutes a day on a free typing site such as Typing.com (see the Resource library). Record your words-per-minute today and again after one week.

# Module: Files, folders and storage
Learn how computers organise information so you never lose your work again.

## Lesson: Files and folders explained (9 min)
Everything you save on a computer — a letter, a photo, a song — is a **file**. To keep files tidy, we put them into **folders** (also called *directories*), just like papers in a cabinet.

### File names and extensions
A file name has two parts separated by a dot: `report.docx`

- `report` is the **name** you choose.
- `.docx` is the **extension**, which tells the computer what *type* of file it is and which program should open it.

| Extension | Type | Opens with |
|---|---|---|
| `.docx` | Word document | Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice |
| `.xlsx` | Spreadsheet | Excel, Google Sheets |
| `.pptx` | Presentation | PowerPoint, Google Slides |
| `.pdf` | Portable document | Any PDF reader or web browser |
| `.jpg`, `.png` | Images | Photos app, browser |
| `.mp3` | Audio | Music player |
| `.mp4` | Video | Video player |
| `.zip` | Compressed folder | File manager (extract first) |

> [!WARNING]
> Be very careful with files ending in `.exe`, `.apk` or `.bat` that arrive by email or WhatsApp. These are programs, and opening them can install viruses.

### Paths: the address of a file
A **path** shows exactly where a file lives, like an address:

```
C:\Users\Ama\Documents\School\SHS1\Maths-assignment.docx
```

Read it from left to right: drive `C:` → `Users` → `Ama` → `Documents` → `School` → `SHS1` → the file.

### Good naming habits
- Use clear, descriptive names: `CV-Kwame-Mensah-2026.pdf`, not `final final 2.pdf`
- Use dashes or underscores instead of spaces for files you will share online
- Put dates as **year-month-day** (`2026-03-15`) so files sort in order
- Avoid symbols like `/ \ : * ? " < > |` — many systems do not allow them

> [!TRY]
> Create a folder called `DBI Learn` in your Documents. Inside it, create folders called `Notes`, `Projects` and `Certificates`. This is where you'll keep your work for this programme.

## Lesson: Organising, finding and backing up your work (10 min)
A tidy computer saves time and stress. Professionals follow a simple system.

### A simple folder structure
```
Documents/
├── School/
│   ├── 2026-Term1/
│   └── 2026-Term2/
├── Work/
│   ├── CV-and-applications/
│   └── Projects/
└── Personal/
    ├── Photos/
    └── Receipts/
```
Keep the top level small (3–6 folders). Go deeper only when a folder gets crowded.

### Moving, copying and deleting
- **Copy** (`Ctrl + C`, then `Ctrl + V`) makes a duplicate; the original stays.
- **Cut** (`Ctrl + X`, then `Ctrl + V`) moves the file.
- **Delete** sends a file to the **Recycle Bin** (Windows) or **Trash** (Mac). You can restore it from there until you empty the bin.
- **Drag and drop** with the mouse moves files between folders on the same drive.

### Finding files fast
- Use the **search box** in File Explorer or the Start menu and type part of the name.
- Sort a folder by **Date modified** to see your newest work first.
- On Android, use the **Files** app and its search.

### Back up — or risk losing everything
Laptops get stolen, phones fall into water, and hard drives fail. A **backup** is a second copy kept somewhere else.

The professional rule is **3-2-1**:

- **3** copies of important files,
- on **2** different types of storage (e.g. laptop + flash drive),
- with **1** copy off-site or in the cloud (e.g. Google Drive, OneDrive).

**Cloud storage** keeps your files on secure servers on the internet. Google gives every account 15 GB free. Files sync automatically between your phone and computer, and you can share a link instead of attaching large files.

> [!TIP]
> Turn on automatic photo backup in Google Photos or OneDrive so your pictures are saved even if you lose your phone.

> [!TRY]
> Copy your three most important files to a flash drive *or* upload them to Google Drive. Congratulations — you now have a backup.

## Lesson: Compressing and sharing files (7 min)
Large files are slow to send and use a lot of data. Compression and smart sharing help.

### Compressing with ZIP
A **ZIP** file packs many files into one smaller file.

- **Windows:** select the files → right-click → **Send to → Compressed (zipped) folder**.
- **Mac:** select → right-click → **Compress**.
- To open a zip, double-click it, then choose **Extract all** so you can edit the files.

Zipping text documents can reduce their size a lot. Photos and videos are already compressed, so zipping them saves little.

### Choosing the right format to share
- Send **PDF** when you want the document to look the same everywhere and not be edited — CVs, certificates, final reports.
- Send **DOCX/XLSX** when the other person needs to edit.
- Reduce photo size before sending: most phones let you choose "standard" instead of "original" quality.

### Sharing a link instead of an attachment
Email attachments are usually limited to about 20–25 MB. For bigger files, upload to Google Drive or OneDrive and share a link. You can choose who can **view**, **comment** or **edit**.

> [!WARNING]
> "Anyone with the link" means exactly that — anyone who gets the link can open the file. Use it only for things that are not private.

> [!TRY]
> Save one of your documents as a PDF (in Word: **File → Save As → PDF**; in Google Docs: **File → Download → PDF**). Compare the file sizes.

# Module: The internet and email
Find reliable information online and communicate like a professional.

## Lesson: How the internet and the web work (9 min)
The **internet** is a worldwide network of computers connected by cables, satellites and radio signals. The **World Wide Web** is one service that runs on the internet — the websites you visit in a **browser** such as Chrome, Firefox, Edge or Safari. Email, WhatsApp and video calls also use the internet but are not "the web".

### How you connect
- **Mobile data** (3G/4G/5G) from MTN, Telecel or AirtelTigo
- **Wi-Fi** from a router at home, school or a café
- **Fibre / fixed broadband** in offices

### Web addresses (URLs)
A URL is the address of a page:

```
https://www.ghana.gov.gh/education/scholarships
```

- `https://` — the **protocol**. The **s** means *secure* (encrypted). Look for the padlock 🔒 in the address bar.
- `www.ghana.gov.gh` — the **domain name**. `.gov.gh` is used by government; `.edu.gh` by schools; `.com` by companies.
- `/education/scholarships` — the specific **page** on that site.

> [!WARNING]
> Scammers create look-alike domains such as `mtn-promo-gh.com` or `ecobank-verify.net`. Always check the real domain before entering passwords or payment details.

### Using a browser well
- **Tabs** let you keep several pages open; `Ctrl + T` opens a new tab.
- **Bookmarks** (`Ctrl + D`) save pages you visit often.
- **History** (`Ctrl + H`) shows pages you visited before.
- **Incognito/private mode** does not save history on that device — useful on shared computers, but it does *not* make you invisible online.

> [!TRY]
> Open your browser, bookmark the DBI Learn website, then open a new tab with `Ctrl + T` and close it with `Ctrl + W`.

## Lesson: Searching smartly and checking sources (10 min)
Search engines like Google and Bing can find almost anything — if you ask well. And because anyone can publish online, you must check what you find.

### Search like a pro
- Use **specific keywords**, not full questions: `WASSCE 2026 timetable` instead of "when will the WASSCE exams start this year please".
- Put exact phrases in **quotes**: `"climate change in Ghana"`
- Exclude words with a minus sign: `jaguar -car`
- Search one website: `scholarships site:edu.gh`
- Find a file type: `business plan template filetype:pdf`

### Is this source trustworthy? Use the **CRAAP** test
- **Currency** — When was it published or updated? Old information may be wrong today.
- **Relevance** — Does it actually answer your question?
- **Authority** — Who wrote it? Are they an expert, a government body, a recognised organisation?
- **Accuracy** — Is it supported by evidence? Do other reliable sources agree?
- **Purpose** — Is it trying to inform, sell something, or make you angry so you share it?

### Spotting fake news
Be suspicious when a message:
- uses ALL CAPS, many exclamation marks or says "forward to 10 people",
- promises free money, free data or a job with no interview,
- has no named source or date,
- uses edited or old photos (try a reverse image search with Google Lens).

> [!TIP]
> Before you share, pause and check. Search the headline and see whether trusted news outlets are reporting the same thing.

> [!TRY]
> Search for "benefits of drinking water". Pick two results and score each one against the CRAAP test. Which one would you use for a school assignment, and why?

## Lesson: Writing professional emails (10 min)
Email is the main way schools, employers and organisations communicate. A well-written email makes a strong first impression.

### Create a professional address
Use your name: `kofi.boateng@gmail.com` — not `sweetboy4life@...`. Gmail and Outlook are free.

### Parts of an email
- **To** — the main recipient
- **Cc** (carbon copy) — people who should be informed
- **Bcc** (blind carbon copy) — hidden recipients; use it when emailing many people who don't know each other
- **Subject** — a short, clear summary
- **Body** — your message
- **Attachment** 📎 — files you send along

### A professional email template
```
Subject: Application for IT Assistant Internship – Kofi Boateng

Dear Mrs. Asante,

I am writing to apply for the IT Assistant internship advertised on your
website. I am a final-year SHS student and have completed the Digital
Literacy and Web Development courses on DBI Learn.

Please find my CV and certificate attached. I would welcome the
opportunity to discuss how I can support your team.

Thank you for your time.

Kind regards,
Kofi Boateng
+233 24 000 0000
```

### Golden rules
1. Write a **specific subject**.
2. Greet properly: "Dear Mr./Mrs./Dr. Surname" or "Hello Ama" for people you know.
3. Keep it **short** — say why you're writing in the first two lines.
4. **Attach before you write** "please find attached" — it's easy to forget!
5. Re-read for spelling and tone before you click **Send**.
6. Reply within 24–48 hours when someone writes to you.

> [!WARNING]
> Never send your passwords, PINs or mobile-money codes by email. No genuine bank, school or company will ask for them.

> [!TRY]
> Write an email to a teacher or mentor asking for advice on which DBI Learn course to take next. Use the template above.

# Module: Staying safe and productive
Protect your accounts and get real work done with documents, spreadsheets and slides.

## Lesson: Passwords, 2-step verification and scams (10 min)
Your email and social media accounts are keys to your digital life. If someone steals your email password, they can reset the passwords of almost everything else.

### Make passwords strong — and different
A strong password is **long** and **unique**. The easiest way is a **passphrase**: several random words together.

- Weak: `ama123`, `password`, `12345678`, your birthday
- Strong: `Mango-Bicycle-Rain-47!`

Never reuse the same password on different sites. If one site is hacked, criminals try that password everywhere. A **password manager** (such as Bitwarden or the one built into Chrome/Google) can remember them for you.

### Turn on 2-step verification (2SV)
**Two-step verification** asks for a second proof — a code sent to your phone or an authenticator app — after your password. Even if someone steals your password, they cannot get in without your phone. Turn it on for email, WhatsApp, Facebook and banking apps.

> [!TIP]
> In WhatsApp, go to **Settings → Account → Two-step verification** and set a PIN. This stops criminals from taking over your account.

### Common scams in Ghana and how to spot them
- **Mobile money reversal scam:** "I sent money to your number by mistake, please send it back." Check your actual MoMo balance — usually no money arrived.
- **Fake promotions:** "You've won GH₵5,000! Send your PIN to claim." Real promotions never ask for your PIN.
- **Phishing links:** messages pretending to be from your bank or school with a link to "verify your account".
- **Job scams:** jobs that ask you to pay a "registration fee" first.

**The golden rule:** never share your PIN, password or one-time code with anyone — not even someone who says they are from MTN, the bank or DBI.

> [!TRY]
> Change the password of your main email to a passphrase and switch on 2-step verification today.

## Lesson: Documents, spreadsheets and presentations (10 min)
Three types of "office" programs cover most school and work tasks. Free options include **Google Docs/Sheets/Slides** (online, with offline mode), **Microsoft 365 web** and **LibreOffice** (free download).

### Word processors — for writing
Use **Word** or **Google Docs** for letters, essays, CVs and reports.

- Use **Heading 1 / Heading 2** styles for titles — this lets you create an automatic table of contents.
- Use **bullet points** for lists.
- Insert **page numbers** and use **spell check** (red underlines).
- Export to **PDF** before sending final versions.

### Spreadsheets — for numbers and lists
Use **Excel** or **Google Sheets** for budgets, marks, inventories and data. A spreadsheet is a grid of **cells** named by column letter and row number (`B3`). You can write **formulas** that calculate automatically:

```
=SUM(B2:B10)      adds the numbers from B2 to B10
=AVERAGE(C2:C30)  finds the average mark
```

You'll go much deeper in our **Spreadsheets & Data Analysis** course.

### Presentations — for sharing ideas
Use **PowerPoint** or **Google Slides** for class presentations and pitches. Follow the **1–6–6 rule**: one idea per slide, no more than six bullet points, no more than six words per bullet. Use large images and few words — you are the presenter, not the slides.

> [!TIP]
> Google Docs, Sheets and Slides save automatically and let several people edit the same file at once — perfect for group assignments.

> [!TRY]
> Create a one-page document introducing yourself: your name, school, three skills you want to learn, and your dream job. Use a heading and a bullet list, then download it as a PDF.

## Lesson: Healthy, responsible digital habits (8 min)
Technology should help you, not harm you. Good digital citizens protect their health, their privacy and other people.

### Your digital footprint
Everything you post online leaves a **digital footprint**. Employers and universities often search applicants' names. Before posting, ask: *Would I be happy for my teacher, parent or future boss to see this?*

- Set social media accounts to **private** if you don't need them public.
- Don't post your exact location, school timetable or home address.
- Think twice before sharing photos of other people — ask permission.

### Respect and online kindness
Treat people online as you would face to face. **Cyberbullying** — insulting, threatening or embarrassing someone online — causes real harm. If you are bullied: don't reply, take screenshots, block the person and tell a trusted adult or teacher.

### Copyright and plagiarism
Copying someone else's work and presenting it as your own is **plagiarism**. Always credit sources, and use images that are free to use (for example from Unsplash or Pexels).

### Look after your body
- Follow the **20-20-20 rule**: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.
- Sit with your back supported and the screen at eye level.
