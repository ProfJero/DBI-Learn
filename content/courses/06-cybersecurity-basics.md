# Module: Understanding cyber threats
Learn who attackers are, what they want and how attacks happen.

## Lesson: Why cybersecurity matters (8 min)
**Cybersecurity** means protecting computers, phones, networks and data from attack, damage or unauthorised access. As more of life moves online — banking, school, shopping, government services — it affects everyone.

### The CIA triad
Security professionals think about three goals:

| Goal | Meaning | Example of a failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Confidentiality** | Only the right people can see information | Your exam results or medical records are leaked |
| **Integrity** | Information is accurate and hasn't been tampered with | Someone changes the amount on a payment |
| **Availability** | Systems and data work when you need them | A bank's app is down during a cyberattack |

### Who are the attackers?
- **Cybercriminals** — want money: scams, fraud, stealing and selling data, ransomware.
- **Hacktivists** — attack for political or social causes.
- **Insiders** — employees or people you know who misuse their access.
- **State-sponsored groups** — work for governments to spy or disrupt.
- **Opportunists** — not always experts; they use ready-made tools and target easy victims.

### You are a target — even if you're "not important"
Many people think "I have nothing worth stealing." But attackers want:
- your **mobile money and bank balance**,
- your **accounts** (to scam your contacts in your name),
- your **personal data** (to commit identity fraud),
- your **device** (to use in larger attacks).

Most attacks are automated and target thousands of people at once. Good habits make you a harder target, and attackers move on to easier ones.

> [!NOTE]
> Ghana has a **Cyber Security Authority (CSA)** that receives reports of cybercrime. Reporting helps protect others too.

## Lesson: Malware, ransomware and how infections happen (9 min)
**Malware** (malicious software) is any program designed to harm you or your device.

### Common types of malware
| Type | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Virus** | Attaches to files and spreads when they're shared |
| **Worm** | Spreads by itself across networks |
| **Trojan** | Pretends to be useful software (a "free" game or cracked app) but contains malware |
| **Spyware** | Secretly watches what you do — messages, passwords, location |
| **Adware** | Floods you with ads and slows your device |
| **Ransomware** | Locks or encrypts your files and demands payment to unlock them |

### How devices get infected
- Installing apps from **outside official stores** (APK files shared on WhatsApp or websites)
- Downloading **pirated** software, films or games
- Opening **email attachments** from unknown senders
- Clicking **links** in suspicious messages
- Plugging in **infected flash drives** — very common in shared computer labs and print shops
- Using outdated software with known security holes

### Signs your device might be infected
- Suddenly very slow, hot, or the battery drains quickly
- Pop-up ads everywhere, even outside the browser
- Apps you didn't install
- Data bundle finishing much faster than usual
- Friends receiving strange messages from you

### Ransomware: why backups matter
Ransomware attacks have shut down hospitals, schools and businesses worldwide. Paying doesn't guarantee you'll get your files back. The best protection is **regular backups** stored separately — then you can restore without paying.

> [!WARNING]
> "Cracked" or "free premium" versions of paid apps are one of the most common ways malware spreads. If it's normally paid and someone offers it free, assume there's a catch.

> [!TRY]
> On your phone, open **Settings → Apps** and review the list. Uninstall any app you don't recognise or no longer use.

## Lesson: Social engineering — hacking humans (9 min)
**Social engineering** means tricking people into giving away information or doing something unsafe. It's often easier to fool a person than to break into a computer.

### The attacker's psychological tricks
- **Urgency** — "Your account will be blocked in 30 minutes!"
- **Authority** — pretending to be a bank, telecom, police officer, school head or boss.
- **Fear** — "There's a warrant for your arrest unless you pay."
- **Greed / excitement** — "You've won a prize!", "Get rich with this investment."
- **Trust & familiarity** — using a friend's hacked account or knowing your name.
- **Helpfulness** — "I'm from IT support, I need your password to fix your computer."

When you notice any of these feelings being pushed on you, **slow down**. That pressure is a warning sign.

### Common social-engineering attacks
- **Phishing** — fake emails or websites that steal logins (more in the next module).
- **Vishing** — voice phishing: phone calls pretending to be your bank or network.
- **Smishing** — SMS phishing: text messages with fake links or instructions.
- **Pretexting** — inventing a believable story ("I'm the new accountant, please send me the staff list").
- **Baiting** — leaving infected flash drives labelled "Salaries 2026" for someone to plug in.
- **Shoulder surfing** — watching you type your PIN at an ATM or MoMo vendor.

### The golden defence: verify independently
If someone asks for money, a code or information:
1. **Stop** — don't act immediately.
2. **Verify** using a contact you already trust — call the official number on the back of your card, or call your friend on their usual number.
3. **Never** share PINs, passwords or one-time codes. No genuine organisation will ask for them.

> [!TIP]
> Agree a "family password" with close relatives. If someone calls claiming to be them in trouble and asking for money, ask for the password.

# Module: Protecting your accounts
Lock down your most important accounts with strong passwords and extra verification.

## Lesson: Strong passwords and password managers (9 min)
Weak and reused passwords are behind a huge share of account takeovers.

### How passwords get cracked
- **Guessing** — names, birthdays, phone numbers, football clubs.
- **Brute force** — computers trying billions of combinations; short passwords fall in seconds.
- **Dictionary attacks** — trying common words and leaked passwords (`password123`, `iloveyou`).
- **Credential stuffing** — using passwords leaked from one website to log in to others. This is why **reusing passwords is so dangerous**.

### What makes a password strong?
**Length beats complexity.** A long passphrase is both stronger and easier to remember than a short jumble.

| Password | Rough time to crack |
|---|---|
| `kofi1995` | Instantly (name + year) |
| `K0f!95` | Minutes to hours |
| `Banku-Lantern-Rocket-River` | Centuries |

Guidelines:
- At least **12–16 characters**; a passphrase of **4 random words** is great.
- **Unique** for every important account.
- Never based on personal information others can find.

### Password managers
A **password manager** stores all your passwords in an encrypted vault, protected by one strong master password. It can generate random passwords and fill them in for you.

- Free options: **Bitwarden**, and the password managers built into **Google Chrome/Android** and **Apple** devices.
- You only need to remember **one** strong master passphrase.

### Check for leaks
Visit **haveibeenpwned.com** and enter your email to see whether it appeared in known data breaches. If it has, change the password for that site — and anywhere you reused it.

> [!WARNING]
> Writing your PIN on your MoMo SIM pack or phone case, or sharing it with a vendor "to help", gives criminals easy access.

> [!TRY]
> Check your email on haveibeenpwned.com. Then create a four-word passphrase and use it for your most important account.

## Lesson: Multi-factor authentication (8 min)
**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)** — also called two-factor or two-step verification — requires a second proof of identity in addition to your password.

### The three factors
1. **Something you know** — password, PIN
2. **Something you have** — your phone, a security key
3. **Something you are** — fingerprint, face

Using two different factors means a stolen password alone isn't enough.

### Types of second factor (from weaker to stronger)
- **SMS codes** — better than nothing, but vulnerable to SIM-swap fraud.
- **Authenticator apps** (Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator) — generate codes on your phone; stronger than SMS.
- **Passkeys and security keys** — the strongest option; resistant to phishing.

### Turn it on now
- **Email (Gmail/Outlook):** Account settings → Security → 2-Step Verification. Do this first — your email can reset all your other passwords.
- **WhatsApp:** Settings → Account → Two-step verification → set a 6-digit PIN.
- **Facebook / Instagram:** Settings → Accounts Center → Password and security → Two-factor authentication.
- **Mobile money / banking apps:** enable biometrics and keep your PIN secret.

Save your **backup codes** somewhere safe (on paper, at home) in case you lose your phone.

### SIM-swap fraud
Criminals convince or bribe someone to transfer your phone number to a new SIM card. They then receive your SMS codes and MoMo access. Warning sign: your phone suddenly shows "No service" for a long time. Contact your network immediately.

> [!TIP]
> Any time you receive a login code you didn't request, someone may know your password. Change it immediately.

> [!TRY]
> Turn on two-step verification for your email and WhatsApp today, and store your backup codes safely.

## Lesson: Recognising phishing (10 min)
**Phishing** is when attackers pretend to be a trusted organisation to trick you into revealing information or clicking a malicious link. It's the most common cyberattack in the world.

### Anatomy of a phishing message
```
From: MTN MoMo Security <momo-alert@mtn-secure-verify.com>
Subject: URGENT: Your MoMo wallet will be suspended

Dear Customer,

We detected unusual activity on your account. To avoid suspension
within 24 hours, verify your details now:

https://mtn-momo-verify.gh-login.net

Enter your number and PIN to continue.
```

Red flags:
- 🚩 The sender's domain isn't the real company domain.
- 🚩 Generic greeting: "Dear Customer".
- 🚩 Urgency and threats: "within 24 hours".
- 🚩 A link to a strange domain — the real domain is the part just before the first single `/`. Here it's `gh-login.net`, not MTN.
- 🚩 It asks for your **PIN** — legitimate companies never do.
- 🚩 Spelling or grammar mistakes (but modern phishing can be well written!).

### Check links before you click
- On a computer, **hover** over a link to see where it really goes.
- On a phone, **long-press** the link to preview the address.
- When in doubt, don't click. Open the official app or type the website address yourself.

### Spear phishing
Some attacks are personalised using information from your social media: your name, school, boss's name. These are harder to spot — another reason to keep personal information private.

### What to do with a phishing message
1. Don't click links, download attachments or reply.
2. Report it (in Gmail: **⋮ → Report phishing**; forward scam SMS to your network's reporting number).
3. Delete it.
4. If you already entered a password, **change it immediately** and turn on MFA.

> [!TRY]
> Look at your email spam folder. Find one phishing message and identify at least three red flags.

# Module: Protecting devices, data and privacy
Keep your phone and computer secure, and control what others know about you.

## Lesson: Securing phones and computers (9 min)
Your phone holds your money, messages, photos and identity. Protect it like your wallet.

### Essential device protections
1. **Screen lock** — use a 6-digit PIN or biometrics (fingerprint/face). Simple swipe patterns are easy to guess from smudges on the screen. Never leave your phone unlocked.
2. **Updates** — install system and app updates promptly. They fix security holes that attackers actively exploit.
3. **Official app stores only** — Google Play or the Apple App Store. Turn off "Install unknown apps".
4. **App permissions** — does a torch app really need your contacts and location? Review permissions in Settings and remove unnecessary ones.
5. **Find My Device** — turn on Google's *Find My Device* or Apple's *Find My* so you can locate, lock or erase a lost phone.
6. **Antivirus** — on Windows, keep **Microsoft Defender** switched on (it's built in and free). On Android, Google Play Protect scans apps.
7. **Encryption** — modern phones encrypt data automatically when you set a screen lock.

### Shared and public computers
In internet cafés, libraries and school labs:
- Use a **private/incognito** window.
- Never tick "Remember me" or let the browser save your password.
- Always **log out** and close the browser when done.
- Avoid banking or MoMo on shared computers.

### Flash drives
Scan flash drives with antivirus before opening files, especially ones used at print shops. Never plug in a drive you found lying around.

### If your phone is lost or stolen
1. Use Find My Device to lock or erase it.
2. Call your network provider to **block the SIM** (this protects your MoMo).
3. Change passwords for email and important apps.
4. Report to the police if stolen.

> [!TRY]
> Check that your phone has a screen lock, Find My Device is on, and all updates are installed. Review the permissions of three apps.

## Lesson: Safe browsing and public Wi-Fi (8 min)
### HTTPS and the padlock
Websites starting with `https://` encrypt data between you and the site. Never enter passwords or payment details on a page without HTTPS. But remember: the padlock means the *connection* is private — **not** that the website is honest. Scammers can get padlocks too.

### Public Wi-Fi risks
Free Wi-Fi in hotels, airports and cafés is convenient but risky:
- Attackers can create **fake hotspots** with names like "Free_Airport_WiFi".
- On poorly secured networks, others may be able to see unencrypted traffic.

Safer habits:
- Avoid banking, MoMo and entering passwords on public Wi-Fi — use your mobile data instead.
- Confirm the exact network name with staff.
- Turn off **auto-connect** to open networks.
- A reputable **VPN** encrypts your traffic on public networks.

### Your home Wi-Fi
- Change the router's default admin password.
- Use **WPA2** or **WPA3** security with a strong Wi-Fi password.
- Update the router's software when available.

### Downloads and pop-ups
- Download software only from official websites.
- Close pop-ups that say "Your phone has a virus! Click here" — they're scams.
- Be careful with browser extensions; install only well-known ones.

> [!TIP]
> Bookmark the real websites of your bank and school portal, and always use the bookmark instead of searching or clicking links.

> [!TRY]
> Check your phone's Wi-Fi settings and turn off automatic connection to open networks.

## Lesson: Privacy and your digital footprint (9 min)
**Privacy** is your right to control who knows what about you. Every post, check-in, like and app sign-up adds to your **digital footprint**.

### What attackers can learn from social media
- Your full name, school, workplace and friends
- Your birthday (often used in passwords and security questions)
- Your location and daily routine (photos tagged at home, "Travelling to Accra today!")
- Your family members' names
- Your phone number and email

Combined, this information enables **identity theft**, convincing **spear-phishing**, and even physical risks.

### Privacy checklist for social media
- ✅ Set your profile to **private** or **friends only**.
- ✅ Hide your phone number and email from your public profile.
- ✅ Turn off location tagging on photos.
- ✅ Post holiday photos *after* you return, not while your home is empty.
- ✅ Don't post photos of ID cards, certificates with ID numbers, boarding passes or bank cards.
- ✅ Review which apps are connected to your Facebook or Google account and remove unused ones.
- ✅ Think before sharing others' photos and information — get consent.

### Data protection law
Ghana's **Data Protection Act, 2012 (Act 843)** gives people rights over their personal data and requires organisations to protect it. Organisations should collect only what they need and keep it secure — something to remember if you build apps or run a business.

### Quizzes and "fun" apps
Viral quizzes like "Your stage name is your first pet + street you grew up on" are often harvesting answers to common security questions. Skip them.

> [!TRY]
> Open the privacy settings of one social media account and make three changes that reduce what strangers can see.

# Module: Responding to incidents and your cyber career
Know what to do when things go wrong — and how to build a career protecting others.

## Lesson: What to do if you've been hacked or scammed (9 min)
Even careful people can be caught. Acting quickly limits the damage.

### If an account is hacked
1. **Try to recover it** — use the "Forgot password" option or the platform's account recovery page.
2. **Change the password** to a new, unique one.
3. **Turn on MFA** and remove unknown devices and sessions (Settings → Security → Devices).
4. **Check recovery details** — make sure the recovery email and phone number are yours.
5. **Warn your contacts** that messages from your account may be fake.
6. **Change reused passwords** on other sites.

### If you've lost money to a scam
1. **Call your network or bank immediately** — mobile money providers can sometimes freeze or reverse transactions if reported quickly.
2. **Collect evidence** — screenshots, phone numbers, transaction IDs, dates.
3. **Report to the police** and to Ghana's **Cyber Security Authority** (CSA).
4. Be careful of "recovery scams" — people who promise to get your money back for a fee.

### If your device may be infected
1. Disconnect from the internet.
2. Run a full antivirus scan.
3. Uninstall suspicious apps.
4. If problems continue, back up personal files (photos, documents — not apps) and **factory reset** the device.

### At work or school
Report incidents to the IT team or a teacher right away — even if you think you made a mistake. Quick reporting can stop an attack spreading. Good organisations treat reporting as responsible, not shameful.

> [!TIP]
> Save your network's customer care number and your bank's official fraud line in your contacts now, before you ever need them.

> [!TRY]
> Write a one-page "If I get hacked" plan with the steps and phone numbers you would need. Keep a copy at home.

## Lesson: Security at work and for small businesses (8 min)
Businesses, schools and NGOs hold valuable data: customer details, payments, staff records. Small organisations are frequent targets because they often have weaker defences.

### Key practices for organisations
- **Least privilege** — give each person access only to what they need for their job.
- **Strong authentication** — MFA on email and admin accounts.
- **Regular backups** — automated, tested, with a copy offline or off-site.
- **Updates** — keep all computers, phones and the website updated.
- **Security awareness training** — teach staff to spot phishing; people are the first line of defence.
- **Separate accounts** — don't share logins between staff; remove access when people leave.
- **Payment verification** — confirm any change of supplier bank or MoMo details by phone using a known number. "Invoice fraud" emails often ask to pay a "new account".

### Protecting customer data
- Collect only what you need.
- Store it securely; don't leave customer lists on shared computers or open WhatsApp groups.
- Follow the Data Protection Act.

### Simple policies
Even a small business should write down:
- Who can access which systems.
- How passwords and devices must be protected.
- What to do and who to call when something goes wrong.

## Lesson: Careers in cybersecurity (8 min)
Cybersecurity has a big global skills shortage — millions of unfilled jobs worldwide — and demand in Africa is growing quickly as banks, telecoms, fintechs and governments go digital.

### Roles in cybersecurity
- **Security Analyst / SOC Analyst** — monitors systems for attacks and responds to alerts.
- **Penetration Tester (ethical hacker)** — legally attacks systems to find weaknesses before criminals do.
- **Security Engineer** — designs and builds secure systems and networks.
- **Incident Responder** — investigates and contains attacks.
- **Governance, Risk & Compliance (GRC)** — creates policies and ensures laws and standards are followed.
- **Security Awareness Trainer** — teaches people to stay safe (like this course!).

### Skills to build
- **Networking** — how the internet and networks work (see our Networking & Cloud course).
- **Operating systems** — Windows and Linux.
- **Programming/scripting** — Python is widely used in security.
- **Curiosity and ethics** — security professionals must be trustworthy.

### Learning pathway
1. Strengthen IT fundamentals (this course, networking, Linux basics).
2. Take free courses such as **Cisco Networking Academy's Introduction to Cybersecurity**.
3. Practise legally on training platforms and capture-the-flag (CTF) challenges designed for learning.
4. Consider entry-level certifications such as CompTIA Security+ or ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity (CC).

> [!WARNING]
> Only ever test systems you own or have **written permission** to test. Unauthorised hacking is illegal under Ghana's Cybersecurity Act, 2020 (Act 1038), even if you "don't do any damage".

> [!TRY]
> Research one cybersecurity role that interests you. Write down three skills it needs and one free resource to start learning.
